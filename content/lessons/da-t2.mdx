Statistics is how analysts avoid lying to themselves. Descriptive stats, distributions, and the anatomy of A/B tests are the daily toolkit.

## Mean vs median: when averages lie

Salaries: 9 people earn ₹40k, one founder earns ₹40L.

- Mean = ₹4.36L — "the average salary is 4.36 lakhs!" (technically true, usefully wrong)
- Median = ₹40k — what the typical person earns

**Skewed data (income, prices, session times) → report the median.** The mean is for symmetric data. Interviewers love this question because it separates people who run functions from people who think.

## Spread tells you if the center matters

Standard deviation near zero → the mean describes everyone. Huge SD → the mean describes nobody. Report both: "median ₹40k, IQR ₹36–44k" beats any single number.

## Correlation ≠ causation (say it with examples)

- Ice cream sales correlate with drowning deaths. (Confounder: summer.)
- Countries with more Nobel laureates consume more chocolate. (Confounder: GDP.)

The analyst's job is asking *"what else could explain this?"* before announcing a cause.

## A/B test anatomy

1. **Hypothesis**: "New checkout reduces cart abandonment"
2. **Random split**: A (control) vs B (treatment) — no peeking at segments
3. **Sample size first**: too-small samples produce confident noise
4. **One metric, decided in advance** — metric-shopping after the fact is p-hacking
5. **Significance**: p < 0.05 means "surprising if there were no effect" — not "95% certain it works"

## Practice — do these, don't just read

1. Take any public dataset: compute mean/median/SD; plot the histogram; decide which center to report and why.
2. Find one misleading news chart (truncated axis, cherry-picked window) and write 3 sentences on the distortion.
3. Critique a real A/B test writeup: what's missing (sample size? pre-registered metric? segment peeking?).
