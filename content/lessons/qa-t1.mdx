QA is engineering discipline applied to quality: understanding how software is built (SDLC) and how bugs flow (lifecycle) makes your testing systematic instead of random clicking.

## The bug lifecycle

```
New → Triaged → Assigned → Fixed → Verified → Closed
                    ↑                    |
                    └──── Reopened ←─────┘  (fix didn't work)
```

Your job at both ends: a **reproducible** report at intake, a rigorous verification at close.

## The bug report engineers love

```markdown
Title: Checkout total shows ₹0 when coupon equals order value

Severity: High (blocks purchases)   Priority: P1 (revenue impact)

Steps:
1. Add item worth ₹500 to cart
2. Apply coupon SAVE500 (₹500 off)
3. Proceed to payment

Expected: payable total ₹0 + delivery fee, order places
Actual:   total shows ₹0.00, "Place order" does nothing

Env: Chrome 130, Android 14 · attachment: HAR + screen recording
Reproduced: 5/5 attempts · First seen: build 2.4.1 (worked in 2.4.0)
```

Every field removes a round-trip question. "Reproduced 5/5" and "worked in 2.4.0" (a regression!) are the two lines that make developers take you seriously.

## Severity vs priority — the classic question

- **Severity** = technical impact (data loss > cosmetic glitch)
- **Priority** = business urgency (typo on the pricing page: low severity, HIGH priority)

The interview example: the company logo is misspelled on the homepage — severity 4 (cosmetic), priority 1 (everyone sees it, brand damage, fix today).

## Practice — do these, don't just read

1. Write 10 test cases for a login feature: happy path, boundaries, lockout, XSS attempt.
2. Find a real bug in any public website; file it professionally (steps, environment, evidence).
3. Classify 10 sample bugs by severity AND priority; write one sentence defending each split.
